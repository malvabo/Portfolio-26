Closing the compliance loop between co‑ops and importers

A coffee and cocoa trader wanted to automate the paperwork between African suppliers and European importers. The team had scoped it as a parsing problem. The half nobody was solving was getting a corrected document back from a farmer co‑op on WhatsApp.

Role

Product and design lead, end‑to‑end

Context

Coffee and cocoa, Africa to Europe

Working with

Client ops, engineering, field consultants

Timeline

~8 weeks, kickoff to shipped

TLDR

  • •Reframed the brief. Parsing an expired certificate still leaves the compliance officer chasing a co‑op. The product had to end in a decision and a follow‑up.
  • •Two ends, two worlds. An officer at a desk with hundreds of shipments, and a co‑op secretary on a shared phone with patchy signal who lives in WhatsApp.
  • •No design system to inherit. Status, evidence and escalation had to be defined as primitives, and made legible on a low‑end phone.

The chain

Who is actually in this process

The co‑op, at origin

Rural coffee and cocoa co‑operatives. Low connectivity, shared devices, WhatsApp as the default channel. Paperwork gets photographed on a phone and sent on.

The importer, in Europe

One compliance officer or a whole team. Documents audited one at a time across email, WhatsApp and whatever tools the company already had.

Nothing sat between them. No shared system where a document could be checked, rejected and re‑requested, so every correction was somebody manually chasing somebody else.

Challenge

Most documents that arrive are not usable

Certificates had expired. Issuers were absent from the registry. Photos were too dark or skewed to read. Unrelated documents came bundled into one file. Only a small share landed in a state anyone could act on, and a rejected declaration stalls a container and ties up working capital.

The plan I inherited was to parse data out of the documents and show it to the importer. That was technically reasonable and would not have solved the problem. If the certificate underneath is expired, a perfectly extracted expiry date only tells the officer faster that they now have to go and get a new one from a co‑op who is not reachable by email.

Research

Interviewing users I could not reach

Rural co‑ops are not sitting on a video call, so I ran the research through the in‑country consultants who already work with them face to face. That risks hearing the consultant’s summary instead of the co‑op’s reality, so I kept the questions concrete: what device, what signal, who takes the photo, what happens the day after a rejection. More than 30 interviews on the supplier side, plus sessions with importer compliance teams.

I then ran prioritisation workshops with the client team. That is where the reframe landed. They had arrived with parsing as the answer and the evidence pointed elsewhere.

Reframe

The calls I made

The client was not a software company, so product decisions did not have another owner. I made them and carried them.

The output is a decision, not a set of fields

An officer needs to know whether the container can move. Every document resolves to approve, or send back for a corrected copy. Extraction became a step inside that rather than the thing we shipped.

Close the loop on the channel the co‑op already uses

The piece the original scope missed entirely. A failed document triggers a WhatsApp alert and an email to the supplier with a link straight into a re‑upload. The importer stops being the courier between two ends of the chain.

Hand the schema to whoever owns the rules

V1 defined the extraction schema for you from document types we had seen. Scale broke it, since requirements shift by country and by importer–supplier pair. V2 lets the importer configure it, which also puts the definition of “correct” with the party accountable for it.

Deferred: the learning loop

Feeding corrections back so extraction sharpens per team is the right long‑term move and the wrong first one. It needs confirmed data the product did not have yet.

V1: fixed schema, parse and display
V2: configurable schema, validation, follow‑up

Solution

Configure, extract, validate, follow up

A schema the importer defines

Upload a sample document and we propose the fields worth pulling: certificate ID, issue and expiry dates, issuing body. They keep what matters. The same flow then handles a certificate of origin or a phytosanitary cert without hard‑coding either.

Validation, so the result means something

The system checks what it read. Still in date, issuer recognised, file legible enough to stand behind. Every value links back to its place in the source image, so a doubtful field is one click to confirm.

Validated

In date, issuer recognised, read cleanly. Move the shipment.

Needs review

Expiring soon, or a field the system is unsure about.

Send back

Expired, unreadable, or failing a rule. Triggers the re‑request.

The follow‑up, handled by the system

Where most of the time used to go. Now it fires on its own: WhatsApp to the supplier, email to the primary compliance contact with managers copied, and a page built for one job. The importer sees the request and its state without leaving the shipment.

An inbox organised by shipment

Larger importers run many shipments at once, so the workspace is folders per shipment with a status on every document. The officer works the exceptions instead of opening every attachment in order.

Craft

Building the visual language from scratch

No design system to inherit and no brand to apply, so the interface language had to be defined before it could be used. I worked from the constraint rather than a mood board. An officer scanning a dense list on a monitor needs density and a status readable at a glance. A co‑op secretary on a weak signal needs one screen, one instruction, one large target, and a page that survives images loading slowly or not at all.

Status is the load‑bearing object. Nearly every screen is a list of things in a state, so status had to survive being the only signal on a row and extend to states we had not invented yet. I defined it once as a primitive, and the rest of the product reads from it. The components that repeat are the document row, the field with its source link, the shipment folder and the escalation notice.

Accessibility

Designing for the worst conditions in the chain

The hardest user here is on a shared low‑end phone, on an intermittent connection, possibly in bright daylight, reading a second language, doing this a handful of times a year. If the design only works in good conditions, the loop does not close.

Status never depends on colour alone; every state carries a written label, and I checked contrast against the surfaces the marks actually sit on. The supplier page assumes nothing: one task per screen, large targets, no hover‑dependent controls, text carrying the meaning before images arrive. The officer side is built for keyboard and scale, with review actions reachable without a mouse and status changes announced rather than only shown.

Impact

What shipped

30+

Supplier interviews via in‑country consultants

8 weeks

Kickoff to shipped product

5 teams

Importers onboarded, with their suppliers

The officer moved from opening every attachment to handling only what the system flags, and the chasing they used to do by hand now goes out on its own. For the client, the configurable schema is what made the product sellable past the first few customers.

On measurement, I will be straight: this launched without the instrumentation to claim a number on error rates, so I am not going to invent one. The metric worth moving is bad documents caught before submission.

Reflection

What I took from it

The most useful thing I did was refuse the scope I was given. Parsing was a real request from a paying client and it solved the visible half. Showing the field evidence that the cost sat in the other half is what changed the outcome.

I also could not observe my hardest users, so I designed around what I could establish with certainty about their conditions rather than a persona I had imagined. Low connectivity, a shared phone and WhatsApp are facts, and the whole supplier side follows from them.
