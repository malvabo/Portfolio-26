Designing trust into an AI compliance agent

The brief was “extract the fields.” The real problem was whether a compliance officer could trust a machine with a customs-rejection on the line. I reframed it around confidence, and the verdict model I built became the spine the rest of the Compliance Agent was organised around.

Challenge

Certificates fail quietly, then loudly

Cross-border shipments move on paper: certificates of origin, EUR.1 movement certificates, phytosanitary certificates, ISO and Rainforest Alliance certs. Each has to be consistent and current before it reaches an authority — and the failure mode is invisible until it isn’t.

The stakes I anchored the work to: a single rejected declaration stalls a shipment, ties up working capital, and often traces back to an expired certificate no one caught in time. So the outcome I owned wasn’t “a faster form” — it was fewer bad documents reaching customs. Every design call laddered up to that.

Why the obvious solution was the wrong one. The engineering instinct was to auto-fill the declaration from OCR and move on. But compliance officers are accountable for what they submit; a form that fills itself silently doesn’t save them work, it transfers risk to them without the means to check it. The hard problem was never extraction accuracy — it was trust under accountability.

And I had to design it under real ambiguity: certificates arrive in every layout imaginable, there was no fixed template to lean on and no clean labelled data to tune against. The model would be uncertain, sometimes wrong, and I had to make that legible rather than pretend it away.

Process

Reframing the brief, then the bets I made

I sat with a trade-compliance SME and walked real submissions to find the question worth researching. It wasn’t “how accurate is the OCR” — accuracy varies by document and you can’t promise it. The question that actually predicted whether an officer would adopt this was: “when the machine is wrong, does the officer catch it before customs does?” That reframing set every decision below.

Bet 1 — kill the auto-fill, even though it demoed better

The auto-fill concept looked more magical in a demo. I argued against shipping it: a confident machine that’s silently wrong is the exact failure the officer is accountable for. Choosing what not to build was the highest-leverage call in the project, and I owned the trade-off with the team — less wow, far more trust.

Bet 2 — make confidence the primary object, not a footnote

I reframed extraction around confidence per field. High-confidence values read as calm; anything the agent is unsure of is surfaced and asks for a one-click confirm, with a link back to the exact span in the source. The interface leads with the machine’s doubt instead of burying it — that legibility is what earned officers’ trust.

Bet 3 — answer “can I trust this?” before the field detail

Watching people use it, the first thing they needed wasn’t a field — it was a verdict. So I collapsed the whole document into one tier, driven by issuer recognition, days-to-expiry, and the lowest critical-field confidence. This is where a screen became a model: a single, reusable way to say how much a document can be trusted.

Deferred on purpose — the learning loop

I scoped out the feedback loop that would let confidence sharpen on each team’s document mix. It was the right long-term bet but the wrong first one — it needed volume the product didn’t have yet. Naming it as next, not now, kept the first release shippable and honest.

Solution

Drop, configure, extract — then triage

A configurable extraction schema — built to scale past this one flow

Instead of hard-coding one template, I designed a Schema Builder: a team defines exactly which fields to pull — name, type, plain-language description — so the same flow handles a certificate of origin or a phytosanitary cert without rework. It was a deliberate systems choice: the pattern had to hold across document types the team hadn’t seen yet, not just the ones on the desk that week.

Confidence you can see

Every extracted value carries a confidence score and links back to its exact span in the source document. Click a field, see where it came from. Low-confidence fields are held for a quick human confirm — the machine’s reasoning is inspectable, which is the whole point.

One verdict, three tiers — the model the rest of the agent reused

A validation panel resolves the whole document into a single tier, so the officer knows at a glance whether to approve, review, or escalate. This verdict became the reusable spine: the same three-tier language now organises the agent’s inbox and dashboard, so certification, missing-document follow-up, and payment all speak one triage vocabulary instead of three.

Auto-Validated

Recognized issuer, in date, critical fields high-confidence. Approve and move on.

Needs review

Expiring soon or a low-confidence critical field. A person confirms before submission.

Flagged · Non-Compliant

Unrecognized issuer or a failed check. Routed to exception escalation.

For the user: the confidence-first framing did the real work — officers trusted the agent because it admitted uncertainty instead of hiding it. Attention now goes only where the machine is unsure, not field-by-field across every document.

For the product: the bigger outcome wasn’t the certification screen — it was the verdict model. By the time it shipped it had become the agent’s shared triage vocabulary, so new surfaces inherited a proven pattern instead of each inventing their own. That’s the leverage: one design decision that compounds across the product rather than solving one screen.

Honest on measurement: this launched without the instrumentation to claim a hard error-rate delta, so I’m not going to invent one. What I’d hold myself to next is exactly that — the metric worth moving is bad documents caught before submission, and closing that measurement gap is the first thing I’d fund.

Reflection

The direction this set

The lesson that stuck: in an expert workflow, surfacing doubt beats projecting certainty. The design earned trust by being legibly unsure in the right places, not by pretending to be right everywhere. That’s not a one-flow trick — it’s a stance on how AI should behave inside work people are accountable for.

So I pushed it as a house principle: inspectable AI — every machine claim traceable to its evidence, every uncertainty given a place to be resolved, no silent confidence. It gives the team a multi-quarter direction to build toward: not “add AI to more screens,” but “make the AI’s reasoning legible everywhere it acts.”

What I’d do next is close the loop — feed each human confirmation back so confidence sharpens on the certificate types a given team actually handles — and stand up the measurement I called out above, so the principle is provable, not just persuasive.
