The problem & context

I started with how the operation actually ran, mapping the real document flows, where people got stuck and where errors piled up, so we prioritised from evidence rather than the obvious build.

  • 1.Compliance documentation was high‑volume, repetitive and error‑prone
  • 2.It sat on the critical path for clearing shipments
  • 3.Nothing made the work reusable from one shipment to the next

User needs

“Every shipment is the same paperwork, filled a little differently, and one wrong field holds the container at the port.”

Business goal

Automate the compliance paperwork on the critical path, in a way that fits each importer’s own rules rather than one fixed reading.

The first bet

We built an MVP that automated compliance docs with the rules defined on our side: encode what “valid” looks like, run documents against it, flag what fails. On paper, the right scope, opinionated and useful out of the box.

What testing revealed

Once the team worked through the live MVP, the gaps showed fast, more so with larger importers. At that scale compliance is not one ruleset: it bends with the country and the product, and every serious importer already has its own reading of what a document must contain.

The rules we had built were ours; what customers needed were theirs, with no path to hand‑mapping all of it without turning an MVP into an open‑ended rules‑engineering project.

The pipeline

The pipeline became the unit of the product: a recipe of fields to pull or fill. Save it once and it keeps working on the documents you point it at.

Results come back structured, mapped to the page.

Building an Extract recipe on a real supplier document.

Two flows, not one chain

Testing killed the assumption that extraction and form‑filling were one pipeline. We split them into Extract and Inject, each useful on its own.

Approach

We stopped being the authority on compliance and became the tool importers use to build their own:

  • 1.Mapped the operation first, and let the gaps decide where the first release went
  • 2.Made the pipeline the unit of the product, defined by a recipe and a trigger
  • 3.Split the work into Extract and Inject, each able to stand alone
  • 4.Drafted schemas from a description, so setup was not a blank slate
  • 5.Made pipelines persist, so they could be duplicated and triggered over an API
  • 6.Removed validation and narrowed the surface around the two flows

Not a blank slate: describe the document and the schema drafts itself.

The subtractions

We dropped validation: mapping every rule across every country and product was hard, not what customers asked us to own, and not needed for the MVP to be valuable, so we parked it.

We narrowed the surface: triggers collapsed to the two that are unambiguous, an API request or on demand; the inbox stayed background context, not a place to build; and a vanity “documents processed” count gave way to a plain “last updated.”

Runs, pared back to a plain record of what pipelines have processed.

The craft underneath

The surface sits on a small design system, tokens, components and motion in one place. I animated the parsing‑stage graphics myself, so a document being read has a state of its own, not a dead spinner.

The reading‑the‑document animation, from the design system’s Motion section.

Components, states and rules documented in the system itself, not just built in the app.

Impact

The move that mattered was commercial. A fixed engine sells one reading of the rules; a pipeline importers configure fits each customer’s own, the difference between a demo and something a varied base can run.
