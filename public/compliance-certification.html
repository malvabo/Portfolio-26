Certify: turning compliance certification into a continuous flow

Redesigning how B2B teams reach and hold security certifications — from a once-a-year audit scramble into an always-on readiness workflow.

Role

Lead Product Designer

Timeline

10 weeks

Team

PM, 3 engineers, compliance SME

Platform

Web

TLDR

  • •Outcome: Cut time-to-certification by an estimated 35% by replacing a spreadsheet-driven audit with a live evidence workflow.
  • •Insight: Teams didn't fail audits on effort — they failed on visibility. Nobody knew what was still missing.
  • •Approach: Reframed certification as a continuous status, not a deadline, surfacing readiness in real time.
  • •Result: A single readiness dashboard that maps every control to its owner, evidence, and state.

Challenge

Certification was a fire drill, not a system

For growing SaaS companies, a SOC 2 or ISO 27001 badge unlocks enterprise deals — but earning one meant weeks of chasing screenshots across Slack, email, and spreadsheets, days before the auditor arrived.

The business context: stalled enterprise deals, blocked on a certificate the team couldn't produce fast enough. The user pain: compliance owners had no reliable picture of where they stood, so “are we ready?” had no honest answer until it was too late.

The constraint: this had to work for non-experts. Most of the people gathering evidence weren't compliance professionals — they were engineers and ops managers doing it on top of their day jobs.

Process

Following the evidence trail

I ran 7 interviews across founders, compliance leads, and the engineers who actually upload evidence, then shadowed one live audit end to end. The pattern was consistent: work was happening, but no one could see the whole.

Key finding: the gap was visibility

Teams weren't lazy — they were blind. Controls lived in one tool, evidence in another, and status in someone's head. The anxiety wasn't “doing the work,” it was “knowing what's left.”

Exploration: deadline vs. continuous

I explored two directions — a guided “audit sprint” checklist, and an always-on readiness model. The sprint tested well for a first cert but collapsed on re-certification. Continuous won.

Iteration: from list to map

Early concepts were flat checklists. Testing showed people couldn't tell who owned what. I reworked the model around ownership and state, so every control answered three questions at a glance: who, what, done?

Solution

One live view of readiness

Control-to-evidence mapping

Each requirement is a card linking its owner, the evidence that satisfies it, and a live state — met, at risk, or missing. The audit is never a surprise because the picture is always current.

Readiness score

A single percentage answers “are we ready?” honestly, in real time, and shows exactly which controls are dragging it down.

Owner-first design

Built for non-experts: plain-language control descriptions, one-click evidence requests, and nudges routed to the person who can actually close the gap — not a generic queue.

Reflection

What I'd carry forward

The pivotal moment was reframing the problem: certification isn't an event to survive, it's a state to maintain. Designing for the second certification, not just the first, changed every decision that followed.

What I'd do differently: I'd test the readiness score's emotional read sooner — a dropping percentage can feel punishing, and framing it as progress rather than a grade matters more than I first assumed.

The project sharpened how I design for non-expert users inside expert domains: lead with plain language, route work to the right owner, and make the invisible state visible before anyone has to ask.
