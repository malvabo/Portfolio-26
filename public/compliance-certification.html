Designing trust into an AI compliance agent

The brief said “extract the fields.” The real problem was trust: would a compliance officer rely on a machine when a customs rejection is on the line? I reframed the work around confidence, and the verdict model I built ended up organising the rest of the Compliance Agent.

Challenge

Certificates fail quietly, then loudly

Cross-border shipments move on paper: certificates of origin, EUR.1 movement certificates, phytosanitary certificates, ISO and Rainforest Alliance certs. Each one has to be current and internally consistent before it reaches an authority. When it isn’t, nobody finds out until the declaration has already been rejected.

I anchored the work to what a rejection actually costs. One rejected declaration stalls a shipment and ties up working capital, and it usually traces back to an expired certificate nobody caught in time. So the outcome I owned wasn’t “a faster form.” It was fewer bad documents reaching customs, and every design decision answered to that.

The obvious build was to auto-fill the declaration from OCR and move on. But compliance officers are personally accountable for what they submit. A form that fills itself silently doesn’t save them work; it just hands them the risk with no way to check it. So the hard part was never OCR accuracy. It was whether someone on the hook would trust the result.

I also had to design this without much to stand on. Certificates arrive in every layout you can imagine, there was no fixed template to code against, and no clean labelled data to tune the model on. It was going to be uncertain and sometimes wrong, and my job was to make that legible instead of hiding it.

Process

Reframing the brief, then the bets I made

I sat with a trade-compliance SME and walked through real submissions to find the question worth researching. It wasn’t “how accurate is the OCR,” because accuracy swings by document and you can’t promise it. The question that actually predicted whether an officer would adopt this was simpler: when the machine gets something wrong, does the officer catch it before customs does? That reframing drove everything below.

Bet 1: kill the auto-fill, even though it demoed better

The auto-fill concept looked more impressive in a demo, and I argued against shipping it anyway. A confident machine that’s quietly wrong is the exact thing the officer gets blamed for. Deciding what not to build was the highest-leverage call in the project, and I owned that trade-off with the team: less wow, a lot more trust.

Bet 2: make confidence the main object, not a footnote

I put confidence per field at the centre of the screen instead of tucking it away. High-confidence values stay calm and quiet. Anything the agent is unsure about gets surfaced and asks for a one-click confirm, with a link straight back to the exact spot in the source document. Leading with the machine’s doubt, rather than burying it, is what won the officers over.

Bet 3: answer “can I trust this?” before the field detail

Watching people use it, the first thing they reached for wasn’t a field. It was a verdict: can I trust this document at all? So I collapsed the whole document into a single tier, driven by whether the issuer is recognised, how many days are left before expiry, and the lowest confidence among the critical fields. That’s the point where a screen turned into a model: one reusable way to say how far a document can be trusted.

Deferred on purpose: the learning loop

I deliberately left out the feedback loop that would let confidence sharpen on each team’s own mix of documents. It’s the right long-term bet and the wrong first one, because it needs a volume of data the product didn’t have yet. Calling it next instead of now kept the first release both shippable and honest.

Solution

Drop, configure, extract, then triage

A configurable extraction schema, built to scale past this one flow

Rather than hard-code a single template, I designed a Schema Builder. A team defines exactly which fields to pull, giving each one a name, a type, and a plain-language description, so the same flow handles a certificate of origin or a phytosanitary cert with no rework. That was a deliberate systems choice. The pattern had to hold up for document types the team hadn’t seen yet, not just the ones sitting on the desk that week.

Confidence you can see

Every extracted value carries a confidence score and links back to its exact span in the source. Click a field and you see where it came from. Low-confidence fields get held back for a quick human confirm before anything moves. The machine’s reasoning stays open to inspection, which is the whole point.

One verdict, three tiers, and the model the rest of the agent reused

A validation panel resolves the whole document down to one tier, so an officer can tell at a glance whether to approve, review, or escalate. That verdict became the reusable spine of the product. The same three-tier language now runs the agent’s inbox and dashboard too, so certification, missing-document follow-up, and payment all speak one triage vocabulary instead of three separate ones.

Auto-Validated

Recognized issuer, in date, critical fields high-confidence. Approve and move on.

Needs review

Expiring soon or a low-confidence critical field. A person confirms before submission.

Flagged · Non-Compliant

Unrecognized issuer or a failed check. Routed to exception escalation.

Impact

What actually shipped, and what stuck

1 model

Verdict language reused across inbox, dashboard & certification

Any layout

One schema, no fixed template. Scales to unseen document types

0 silent

Low-confidence fields never auto-pass to a customs submission

For the officer: the confidence-first framing did the real work. People trusted the agent because it admitted when it wasn’t sure, instead of papering over it. Attention now goes only to the places the machine flags, not field by field across every document.

For the product: the bigger result wasn’t the certification screen. It was the verdict model. By the time it shipped, it had become the agent’s shared way of triaging work, so new surfaces inherited a proven pattern instead of inventing their own. That’s where the leverage is: one design decision that keeps paying off across the product instead of solving a single screen.

On measurement, I’ll be straight: this launched without the instrumentation to claim a hard number on error rates, so I’m not going to invent one. The metric worth moving is bad documents caught before submission, and standing up that measurement is the first thing I’d fund next.

Reflection

The direction this set

The lesson that stuck: in expert work, showing your doubt beats projecting certainty. The design earned trust by being openly unsure in the right places, not by pretending to be right everywhere. That isn’t a trick for one flow. It’s a position on how AI should behave inside work that a person is accountable for.

So I pushed it as a house principle and called it inspectable AI: every machine claim traces back to its evidence, every uncertainty has a place to get resolved, and nothing passes on silent confidence. It gives the team something to build toward over several quarters. Not “add AI to more screens,” but “make the AI’s reasoning legible everywhere it acts.”

What I’d do next is close the loop, feeding each human confirmation back so confidence sharpens on the certificate types a given team actually handles, and stand up the measurement I flagged above so the principle is provable rather than just persuasive.
