Closing the compliance loop between co‑ops and importers

A coffee and cocoa trader wanted software to automate the paperwork between African suppliers and European importers. The team had scoped it as a parsing problem. Sitting with the people on both ends showed that parsing was the easy half, and that the half nobody was solving was getting a corrected document back from a farmer co‑op on WhatsApp. I led product and design, and rebuilt the brief around that.

Role

Product lead and design lead, owned end‑to‑end

Context

Coffee and cocoa, African suppliers to European importers

Working with

Client operations, engineering, in‑country field consultants

Timeline

~8 weeks, kickoff to shipped product

  • •Reframed the brief. The team wanted to parse fields out of certificates and show them to the importer. Parsing an expired certificate still leaves the compliance officer chasing a co‑op. The product had to end in a decision and a follow‑up, not a filled form.
  • •Designed for two very different ends of one chain. A compliance officer at a desk with hundreds of shipments, and a co‑op secretary on a phone with patchy signal who lives in WhatsApp and does not watch an inbox.
  • •Gave the schema away. The first version parsed against document types we already knew. Scale broke that, so importers and suppliers now define the fields that matter to them, and we suggest a starting set from a sample document.

The chain

Who is actually in this process

The client ran supply operations across Africa and Europe and wanted to widen the services they offered. Before I could design anything I had to map who touches a shipment’s paperwork, because the two ends of the chain work in completely different conditions.

The co‑op, at origin

Rural coffee and cocoa co‑operatives. Low connectivity, shared devices, WhatsApp as the default channel. No one is monitoring an email inbox. Compliance paperwork gets photographed on a phone and sent on.

The importer, in Europe

A compliance officer or a compliance team, depending on the size of the importer. Documents get audited one at a time across email, WhatsApp threads and whatever tools each company already had. Almost none of it was automated.

Nothing sat between them. There was no shared system where a document could be checked, rejected and re‑requested, so every correction round trip was somebody manually chasing somebody else.

Challenge

Most documents that arrive are not usable

When an importer wants to move a container, a stack of compliance documentation has to come with it. What actually arrived was rarely clean. Certificates had expired. The issuing organisation was not registered in the relevant registry. Photos were too dark or too skewed to read. Several unrelated documents came bundled into one file. Only a small share landed in a state anyone could act on.

I anchored the work to what that costs. A rejected declaration stalls a container and ties up working capital, and the cause is usually something small that nobody caught in time. So the outcome I owned was fewer bad documents reaching the point of no return, and every decision below answers to that.

The plan I inherited was to parse data out of the documents and pass it to the importer. We had enough suppliers and enough historical documents to make that technically reasonable. It just would not have solved the problem. If the certificate underneath is expired, a perfectly extracted expiry date only tells the officer, faster, that they now have to go and get a new document from a co‑op who is not reachable by email.

Research

Interviewing users I could not reach directly

The people whose behaviour decided whether this product worked were the hardest to talk to. Rural co‑ops are not sitting on a video call. So I ran the research through the in‑country consultants who already work with them face to face, briefing them on what I needed to learn, structuring the conversations, and debriefing after each round.

That runs a real risk of hearing the consultant’s summary instead of the co‑op’s reality, so I kept the questions concrete. What device, what signal, who physically takes the photo, what happens the day after a document gets rejected. Across the project that came to more than 30 interviews on the supplier side, alongside sessions with importer compliance teams.

I then ran workshops with the client team to set priorities against what we had found. That is where the reframe landed. The team had arrived with parsing as the answer, and the evidence pointed somewhere else.

Reframe

The calls I made, and the one I deferred

The client was not a software company, so product decisions did not have another owner. I made them and carried them, which is the part of this project I learned the most from.

Bet 1: the output is a decision, not a set of fields

A compliance officer does not need a form filled in. They need to know whether this container can move. So the product resolves every document into one of two ends: approve and let the shipment proceed, or send it back for a corrected copy. Extraction still happens, but it became a step inside that, not the thing we shipped.

Bet 2: close the loop on the channel the co‑op already uses

This is the piece the original scope was missing entirely. When a document fails, the system contacts the supplier directly: a WhatsApp alert and an email to the primary compliance contact, copying the compliance managers, with a link that opens straight into a re‑upload. The importer stops being the courier between the two ends of the chain.

Bet 3: hand the schema to the people who own the rules

The first version defined the extraction schema for you, based on document types we had seen before. The more we scaled, the more that broke. Requirements shift by country, and a given importer and supplier pair can need something different again. Rather than keep chasing every variant, I moved to a schema the importer configures, with suggested fields from a sample document. It also put the definition of “correct” with the party who is accountable for it.

Deferred on purpose: the learning loop

Feeding every human correction back so extraction sharpens on each team’s own document mix is the right long‑term move and the wrong first one. It needs a volume of confirmed data the product did not have yet. Calling it next rather than now kept the first release shippable and honest.

Solution

Configure, extract, validate, follow up

A schema the importer defines

An importer uploads a sample document and we propose the fields worth pulling from it: certificate ID, issue and expiry dates, issuing body, the details specific to their trade lane. They keep what matters and drop what does not. The same flow then handles a certificate of origin or a phytosanitary certificate without us hard‑coding either one.

Validation, so the result means something

Extraction on its own only moves text around. The system checks what it read: is this certificate still in date, is the issuer recognised, is the file legible enough to stand behind. Every value links back to where it came from in the source image, so an officer can confirm a doubtful field in one click instead of reopening the original.

Validated

In date, issuer recognised, fields read cleanly. Approve and move the shipment.

Needs review

Expiring soon, or a field the system is not confident about. A person confirms it.

Send back

Expired, unreadable, or failing a rule the importer set. Triggers the re‑request.

The follow‑up, handled by the system

A send‑back is where most of the time used to go. Now it fires automatically: WhatsApp to the supplier, email to the primary compliance contact with the compliance managers copied, and a link that opens a page built for one job, replacing this document. The importer sees the request go out and its state, without leaving the shipment.

An inbox organised by shipment

Larger importers run many shipments against many suppliers at once, so the workspace is built around folders per shipment. Documents come in, get processed, and carry a status. The officer works the exceptions instead of opening every attachment in order, which is what the email and WhatsApp version of this job forced them to do.

Craft

Building the visual language from scratch

There was no existing design system here and no brand to inherit, so the interface language had to be defined before it could be applied. That was more freedom than most projects give you and more responsibility, because every inconsistency would be one I introduced.

I worked from the constraint rather than from a mood board. Two audiences, two devices, two very different levels of attention. A compliance officer scanning a dense list on a monitor needs information density and a status they can read at a glance. A co‑op secretary on a phone with a weak signal needs one screen, one instruction, one large target, and a page that still works when images load slowly or not at all.

Status is the load‑bearing object. Almost every screen in the product is a list of things in a state, so the status treatment had to survive being the only signal on a row, stay legible at small sizes, and extend to states we had not invented yet. I defined it once as a primitive with a colour, a label and a shape, and everything else in the product reads from it.

The primitives I set are the ones the product actually repeats: the document row with its status, the field with its source link and confirm action, the shipment folder, and the escalation notice. Getting those four right meant new screens could be assembled rather than designed from nothing.

Accessibility

Designing for the worst conditions in the chain

Accessibility here was not a compliance checklist bolted on at the end. The hardest user in this product is on a shared low‑end phone, on an intermittent connection, possibly in bright daylight, reading a second language, doing this task a handful of times a year. If the design only works in good conditions, the loop I built does not close.

Status never depends on colour alone. Every state carries a written label alongside its colour, because the whole product hangs off status and a colour‑only signal fails for colour‑blind users and in poor light equally. I checked contrast for text and for the status marks against the surfaces they actually sit on, not against pure white.

The supplier page assumes nothing. One task per screen, large touch targets, no hover‑dependent controls, and text that carries the meaning so the page still makes sense before images arrive. The action a co‑op needs is the largest thing on the screen.

The officer side is built for keyboard and for scale. Working an inbox of hundreds of documents is repetitive work, so the review actions are reachable without a mouse, focus order follows the way the list is read, and status changes are announced rather than only shown.

Impact

What shipped

Importers onboarded, along with the suppliers they work with

For the compliance officer, the work moved from opening every attachment to handling only what the system flags. The follow‑up they used to do by hand, chasing a co‑op across WhatsApp and email for a replacement certificate, now goes out on its own and reports back.

For the client, the configurable schema is what made the product sellable past the first few customers. Every new trade lane no longer needed us to model its documents first.

On measurement, I will be straight: this launched without the instrumentation to claim a hard number on error rates, so I am not going to invent one. The metric worth moving is bad documents caught before submission, and standing that up is the first thing I would fund next.

Reflection

What I took from it

The most useful thing I did on this project was refuse the scope I was given. Parsing was a real request from a client who was paying, and it was solving the visible half of the problem. Saying so, and then showing the evidence from the field that the other half was where the cost sat, is the part that changed the outcome.

The second lesson is about designing for people you cannot observe. I could not sit next to a co‑op secretary, so I designed around what I could establish with certainty about their conditions rather than around a persona I had imagined. Low connectivity, a shared phone and WhatsApp are facts. Everything the supplier side does follows from those.

What I would do next is close the learning loop I deferred, so extraction improves on each importer’s own document mix, and put in the measurement that would let the next version of this page carry a number instead of a description.
